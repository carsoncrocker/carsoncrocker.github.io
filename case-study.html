How I built the system

How the system works, and what I learned.

Over three weeks I built a research process around off-the-shelf AI tools, mainly Claude Code with ChatGPT as a critic, for making progress on open math problems: choosing the problems, exploring them, checking every result and deciding what is ready to release. This page explains how it works and the mistakes that shaped it. The results themselves are on the Results page, and the dated events are in the Log.

Why I started

I work in the corporate world, and I'm interested in how progress actually gets made on hard open problems: which problems to pick, how to search, and when to trust a result. I'm not a mathematician, and Claude and ChatGPT did almost all of the mathematics.7 A few of the directions that worked were mine as I learned. On one problem, after hours of symmetric search, I suggested looking at asymmetric configurations instead, and a check along those lines found a smaller example within two minutes.7a I wanted to see how far a good method could take them.

It started, honestly, with a tweet. Someone had asked an AI to “do a breakthrough” on an unsolved problem. I laughed, and then thought it probably could.

My first attempts went nowhere. I aimed at a famous problem and asked the AI for a big result directly.89 That was the first lesson: the AI is a powerful engine, but it needs a method around it. So I built one. Claude writes the code, runs the searches and produces the certificates; ChatGPT critiques. I set the goals and the process, push past the AI's stopping points, run the infrastructure, cross-check the results and decide what is ready to release.10

The system

Claude runs every stage: it generates the questions, launches parallel lines of attack, writes its own checkers, attacks its own results and reports back. ChatGPT and other models act as critics and outside checkers.10 I designed the process they work in, I correct it, and I decide what is real enough to go out.

The working rules, in short. Each is explained, with evidence, below.

  1. Choose problems a search can move and a program can check, and check the literature before spending any compute. Choose
  2. When something is expensive, find a cheaper way to answer the same question instead of spending more compute. Explore
  3. When progress stalls, ask many different small questions in parallel, each tested by a short probe with a one-line verdict. Explore
  4. Grade every search with the rigorous checker, never its own score; nothing counts as verified until something that didn't produce it has checked it. Verify
  5. Push past the point where the AI recommends stopping. Explore
  6. Every claim and mistake goes into an append-only ledger that every session reads first. The ledger
  7. Experts first, credit always, and claim only what has been checked. Release

1. Choose

Choose problems where search and computation can make progress, and where a program can check the answer.

  • Famous problems, which need a deep new idea, went nowhere.9 The method helps most on active questions where a search can contribute: #188 builds on a construction published in June 2026,4 and #189 answers a question Hu and Liu asked in September 2026.11
  • A literature check comes before any computing, and the list of dead targets is checked before one is relaunched.12

2. Explore

Ask many small questions at once, keep the machine busy, and don't stop when the AI wants to.

  • Question storms. When progress stalls, the system asks several different questions in parallel instead of running one search longer. On #188, one such storm produced the idea of letting a small red cell sit very close to a larger one.14
  • Probes. Each idea gets a short test that ends in a one-line verdict. A probe whose positive control never passed counts as uninformative, not as a negative.15
  • Parallel and overnight. Several Claude Code sessions and agents run at once, and runs continue overnight; the #189 certificate came from one such night.16
  • Cheaper, not longer. When a run is expensive, the system looks for a cheaper way to answer the same question instead of more compute. The tools themselves are a target: one rebuilt search tool for #188 ran 4.3 times faster than the one it replaced,17 and each drop in the chart above came from a new idea rather than more computing time.6
  • Past the stopping point. On an earlier problem, Claude twice recommended stopping. Both times I asked it to keep going, and the work continued.18

3. Verify

Nothing is true until it has been checked by something that didn't produce it.

  • Honest scoring. A search is graded by the rigorous verifier, not by its own shortcut.21
  • Certificates. Exact or ball arithmetic, and SAT proofs checked by standard tools and by a formally verified checker; a Lean proof where possible.22
  • Independent checks. A second or third verifier written blind in a separate session, an attacker that tries to break the result, and ChatGPT as an outside referee.23
  • Tests of the tests. Deliberately broken inputs must be rejected, or the check doesn't count.24
  • Literature last as well as first. On a note about the chromatic number of 3-space, the AI referees passed the argument, but only a separate literature check, run just before release, found a paper posted the day before that already covered dimensions four and up. The note was cut to the one case that was still new.36

4. Learn

Results and mistakes are turned into written rules.

  • Lessons are written back as rules. This isn't machine learning in the technical sense: the system learns by rewriting its own instructions.27

The ledger

The most useful single thing in the system is a plain text file.

Every AI session starts with no memory of the last one, and a model will confidently "remember" things that never happened. So nothing counts because a model said it. Every claim goes into a ledger with a date, a status and a pointer to the files that prove it.26 The statuses are simple: a result (certified), closed (certified negative), dead (refuted), not new (prior art found), a lead (not yet verified), or open.

Three rules make it work:

  1. It outranks memory. Every session reads the ledger first. If a model's summary and the ledger disagree, the ledger wins.
  2. It is append-only. Mistakes are never erased; corrections are added as new dated rows. That is why this site can show what was caught before publishing: the record was never rewritten.
  3. A claim is only real when its row points to a passing check. That rule is what made the evidence pages on this site possible.

Here is a real row, from the night before #188 was published. An outside review noticed that a "full clean run" of the package had really passed in two separate runs. The fix was a new row, not an edit:

10-06 ~22:45 CORRECTION to the 10-04 22:23 row: release_188_v4
'verify_all.sh full' clean-copy run did NOT pass as one run - it
passed manifest, Claim A x2, Method 2, Method 1 records + recheck,
then stopped at step 5 (drat-trim not on PATH ...). Lower bound
passed in a SEPARATE run (... 4 DRAT + LRAT VERIFIED). Caught by
external review 10-06. Single full run relaunched 22:40 ...

Verbatim, with a local file path removed.33a The single full run passed the next morning.33b

Reading the ledger back is also how the system learned where it can actually help. The method works best on questions where a computer search can contribute and a program can check the answer, often ones that researchers have raised recently, and much less on famous problems that need a deep new idea. The way new problems are chosen changed because of it.911

5. Release

Experts first, credit always, and claim only what has been checked.

  • A 7-point release checklist, which came from asking whether the rules were right, and a 48-hour hold.29
  • Before posting, I write to the mathematicians whose work a result builds on, and credit where each idea came from.30
  • Each result ships with code anyone can re-run, and the AI session logs are archived with checksums, so who did what can be checked.31

What went wrong, and the rule each mistake left

The AI is remarkably good at fooling itself. These are the mistakes I'd most want someone building a similar system to know about. Each links to its evidence.

What happenedThe rule now
A search reported 63 on #188. It had learned to game its own score; the rigorous verifier found runs of at least 68. 2 OctThe verifier itself is the search's score.21
Claude twice recommended stopping on an earlier problem. Both times there was more to find. 21 SepA stall means the method is stuck, not that the answer is reached.18
A proof checker printed VERIFIED on a tampered file, because the file's header no longer matched its contents. 6 OctHeader and body are checked against each other before a proof check counts.33
A tamper test passed when it should have failed: it changed a clause the proof never used. 6 OctTamper tests must change something the proof depends on.24
Results that looked new were already known, one of them found in a 2021 Polymath16 thread within minutes. 20 SepLiterature check before computing and before any claim; the "not new after all" list is kept public.12
A bound was described as "proved in two ways" when the second method had never been run on it. ChatGPT refused to sign. 4 OctRun every verifier you cite, on the exact object you claim.34
A release package was described as passing one full clean run. It had passed in two separate runs. 6 OctCorrected in the ledger, then re-run as one clean run, which passed.35

What I learned

Most of what I learned is about working with AI, not math. It is remarkably good at exploring once the process is right, and remarkably good at fooling itself. My job turned out to be building the process and not believing anything until it was checked.

If you see something I got wrong, or want to try this in your own field, I'd like to hear from you through my GitHub profile. I'm glad to walk teams through the full workflow, including how the agents are organised and kept honest.
