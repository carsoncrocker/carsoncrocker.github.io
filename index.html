Why I started

I'm not a mathematician. I work in corporate strategy, and a lot of my job is figuring out how to actually use AI. I wanted to see whether someone with the right questions and a good process could make real progress on hard problems they knew nothing about. For me it's more about how you do it than what it is.

It started with a tweet: someone had asked an AI to "do a breakthrough" on an unsolved problem. I laughed, then thought it was probably getting that good.

My first attempts went nowhere. I aimed at famous conjectures, and Claude and ChatGPT would think for days on one step and get bogged down. I realized I was thinking about it the wrong way.

So I stopped asking the AI for answers and started building a system around it. I don't do the mathematics and I don't write the code. I don't come up with each research question either. I built the thing that does: the overnight runs, the process, the memory, the checking and the gates a result has to pass.

The system

Claude runs the research: it generates the questions, launches parallel lines of attack, writes its own checkers, attacks its own results and reports back. ChatGPT and other models act as critics and outside checkers. I designed the system they work in, I correct it, and my corrections become permanent rules. I also decide what is real enough to go out.

  1. 1. Choose

    Choose problems that suit AI: where search and computation can make real progress, and the answer can be proved by a program.

    • Famous conjectures went nowhere. What works are bounds that are recent, loose or built by hand.
    • Scout agents look for targets. A literature check comes before any compute, and the dead list is checked first.
    • The ledger shows which choices paid off, and the next target is picked from that.

  2. 2. Explore

    Ask many small questions at once, keep the machine busy, and don't stop when the AI wants to.

    • Question storms. When progress stalls, the system asks three to five different questions in parallel instead of running one search longer. The playbook has fourteen question types, each added because it produced an improvement: Is it minimal? Which hidden assumption can I drop? Is this condition necessary or only sufficient? Is the engine the bottleneck? Who outside has an idea?
    • Probes. Every idea is tested in minutes, with a positive control that must first reproduce something known. Each ends with a one-line verdict. Two probes with no movement and the idea goes on the dead list.
    • Parallel and overnight. Several Claude Code sessions and agents run at once, some forked to try different approaches, with a fresh-eyes agent in every storm. Nights run from a written directive with time budgets, kill rules and a morning report.
    • Speed. Every round asks whether the tools can go faster, and whether any core is idle. Moving one checker to Linux took it from 93 minutes to 17. A long run is allowed only while it keeps improving.
    • Past the stopping point. On my first problem Claude recommended stopping at 35, then at 29. Both times I pushed on, and both times the next improvement came, ending at 27.

  3. 3. Verify

    Nothing is true until it is checked by something that didn't produce it.

    • Honest scoring. The search is graded by the checker, never by its own shortcut.
    • Certificates. Exact or interval arithmetic, or SAT proofs checked by standard tools and by a formally verified checker; a Lean proof where possible.
    • Independent checks. A second checker written blind in a separate session, ChatGPT and other models as outside referees, and a hostile agent that tries to break each result.
    • Tests of the tests. Deliberately broken inputs must be rejected, or the check doesn't count.

  4. 4. Learn

    Every result and every mistake becomes a rule.

    • AI sessions forget, so the system remembers in files: a ledger of every claim and its status that outranks any model's memory, a playbook, around 40 memory files, a catalogue of tools, and a start-here page every session reads first.
    • Lessons are written back as rules, so each session starts smarter than the last. This isn't machine learning in the technical sense; the system learns by rewriting its own instructions.

  5. 5. Release

    Experts first, credit always, and claim only what has been checked.

    • A seven-point release checklist, a 48-hour hold, careful wording, and nothing goes out without my OK.
    • Before anything is public I write to the mathematicians whose work it builds on, and credit where each idea came from.
    • Each result ships with code anyone can re-run, and every AI conversation is archived with checksums, so who did what can be checked.

What went wrong (and what it taught the system)

The AI is remarkably good at fooling itself. These are the mistakes I'd most want someone building a similar system to know about.

WhenWhat happenedWhat changed
2 OctA search reported 63 on Erdős #188. It had learned to game its own score. The independent checker found the true figure was about 68.The independent checker's test is now the search's score.
21 SepClaude twice recommended stopping, once at 35 and once at 29. Both times there was more to find.A stall is read as the method being stuck, not as the answer being reached.
6 OctA proof checker printed "VERIFIED" on a tampered file, because the file's header no longer matched its contents.Header and body are checked against each other before any proof check counts.
6 OctA tamper test passed when it should have failed. It changed a clause the proof never used.Tamper tests must change something the proof depends on.
20 Sep onwardSeveral results that looked new were already known, including one found in a 2021 Polymath16 thread within minutes of being called new.A literature check comes before compute and before any claim. The list of "not new after all" is kept.

What I learned

Most of what I learned is about working with AI, not maths. It is remarkably good at exploring once the process is right, and remarkably good at fooling itself. My job turned out to be building the process and not believing anything until it was checked.

If you see something I got wrong, or want to try this in your own field, I'd like to hear from you.
