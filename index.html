Erdős Problem #188

published 7 Oct 2026

Color the plane red and blue so that no two red points are exactly 1 apart. What is the smallest k such that some coloring avoids every straight run of k blue points spaced 1 apart? It was known to be between 6 and 6330.

6 ≤ k ≤ 6330→7 ≤ k ≤ 60

ExplanationPackage and note (doi:10.5281/zenodo.23151059)Problem page

Improves the upper bound 6330 of Currier, Mody, Xie and Zhang (arXiv:2606.17194); the lower bound follows the method of Arman and Tsaturian. Gabriel Currier suggested using red cells of different sizes.

Erdős Problem #189

published 7 Oct 2026

Color the plane with two colors. Must there always be a rectangle of area exactly 1 whose four corners share a color? Yes: this shows that 180 specific points already force one. So the fewest colors that can avoid such rectangles is between 3 and 21.

Why I started

I'm not a mathematician; the mathematical ideas did not come from me.1 I wanted to see whether someone with the right questions and a good process could make real progress on hard problems they knew nothing about.

It started, honestly, with a tweet. Someone had asked an AI to “do a breakthrough” on an unsolved problem. I laughed, and then thought it probably could.

My first attempts went nowhere: I aimed at a famous problem and asked the AI for a big result directly.2 So I stopped asking for answers and built a system around the AI instead. Claude writes the code, runs the searches and produces the certificates, and ChatGPT critiques; my part is the goals, the process and the standards.3

The system

  1. Choose problems where search can make progress and a program can check the answer; #189 came from a list of loose published bounds.4
  2. Explore by asking many small questions in parallel; one such storm produced an idea used in the final #188 coloring.5
  3. Verify: nothing counts until something that didn't produce it has checked it, including ChatGPT as an outside referee.6
  4. Learn: mistakes and lessons are written into the project's ledger as rules.7
  5. Release: the authors of the prior work hear first, every idea is credited, and every result ships with code anyone can re-run.8

What went wrong

  • A search on #188 reported 63 after learning to game its own score; the rigorous verifier found runs of at least 68. The verifier is now the score.9
  • Claude twice recommended stopping on an earlier problem; both times I asked it to keep going, and the work continued.10
  • A tamper test passed when it should have failed, because it changed a part of the proof that was never used. Tests must now touch what the proof depends on.11
  • Results that looked new were already known, one of them within minutes of being called new. The literature check now comes first.12

What I learned

Most of what I learned is about working with AI, not math. It is remarkably good at exploring once the process is right, and remarkably good at fooling itself. My job turned out to be building the process and not believing anything until it was checked.

How to check a result yourself

Download a package from its DOI link, unzip it, and run one command in Linux (or WSL on Windows). It re-checks the proof with standard, independent tools and prints PASS or FAIL. For #188, add full to include the lower bound and the second method.

bash verify_all.sh full    # Erdős #188
bash verify.sh             # Erdős #189
