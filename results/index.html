Results

What has been proven, and where each result stands.

Six results are computer-checked proofs that improve a published bound on an open problem; five of them have a page that explains them in plain English. One further result is a written proof that depends on a theorem in OpenAI's recent preprint. The four quantum-code packages were established by exhaustive searches using two separately written programs; none has an independently checkable certificate. Every result comes with a full package, with a DOI, that anyone can download and re-check; the three graph results also have a short check-it-yourself kit on their pages.

Certified equilateral sets in ℓpn: 31 points in ℓ829, 55 points in ℓ553, and six further configurations

published 19 Oct 20268

An equilateral set in ℓpn is a set of points that are all the same distance apart in the p-norm. Kusner's question (Guy 1983) asks whether such a set can contain more than n + 1 points. This deposit provides certificates for n + 2 equilateral points in ℓpn at eight pairs (p, n): (8,29), (9,30), (8,30), (7,30), (9,40), (6,40), (5,53) and (5,55). In particular, e(ℓ829) ≥ 31 and e(ℓ553) ≥ 55, where e denotes the maximum cardinality of an equilateral set. Each configuration is the unique zero of the normalised system (some coordinates fixed at given dyadic values) in the certified box. By the implicit function theorem, equilateral sets of the same size also exist for all p in some open interval around each stated value; no explicit interval is certified. No claim is made that these dimensions are the least possible.

Swanepoel proved the n + 1 bound for p = 4 and constructed larger sets for every 1 < p < 2 in sufficiently large dimension. Ge, Xu and Zhou proved the bound throughout 2 ≤ p ≤ 4. Chalmers constructed 58 equilateral points in ℓ556, and Xiong established the existence of larger-than-(n + 1) sets for every p > 4. Chalmers and Ziegler studied linear excess and proved that, for each fixed positive excess over n + 1, the least dimension admitting that excess is p1+o(1) as p tends to infinity. This deposit supplies certificates for the eight specific configurations listed above.

Three separately written certificate checkers use different arithmetic: a Krawczyk test in Arb ball arithmetic, a Newton–Kantorovich contraction test in exact rational arithmetic, and a Krawczyk test in mpmath interval arithmetic. Positive and negative controls are included. The author is not a professional mathematician. The search, certificates, checking programs and drafts of the note were produced using Anthropic's Claude through Claude Code under the author's direction; separate AI agents wrote the checker implementations. No human expert has reviewed the work to date. Checks and corrections are welcome.

An explicit faithful unit-distance graph in R3 of girth 5 and chromatic number 5 (5,942 vertices)

published 19 Oct 20269

This deposit provides a graph with 5,942 vertices and 17,806 edges, of girth 5 and chromatic number 5, together with a faithful unit-distance embedding in R3. Adjacent vertices are at distance exactly 1, non-adjacent vertices are not at distance 1, and all points are distinct. No claim is made that 5,942 is the minimum possible number of vertices.

De Grey (Geombinatorics 35(3), 2026) asked whether 5-chromatic unit-distance graphs in R3 exist with any girth, as is known for 4-chromatic unit-distance graphs in the plane. Tomon's Theorem 1.3 and Corollary 1.4 establish a stronger existence result: for every prescribed g, there is a faithful 5-chromatic unit-distance graph in R2 with girth at least g (arXiv:2610.03517v2). This deposit supplies one explicit, checkable example of girth exactly 5 in R3. An abstract comparison is Royle's 80-vertex graph of girth 5, whose chromatic number 5 was established by Exoo and Goedgebeur (2019).

The deposit gives coordinates to 45 digits and two separately written checkers. Using interval Krawczyk tests (python-flint/Arb), they certify that an exact unit-distance realisation lies within 10−35 of these coordinates. They also check the colourings and the girth, and rigorously check, over all pairs of points, that all points are distinct and that no non-adjacent pair is at distance 1. Separate AI agents produced the checking implementations; an additional review used ChatGPT. The author is not a professional mathematician. The search, construction, certificates and checking programs were produced using Anthropic's Claude through Claude Code under the author's direction. No human expert has reviewed the work to date. Corrections are welcome.

Circuit-level distance of the gross code's syndrome circuit

published 18 Oct 20266

A quantum code is read out by a circuit that repeatedly measures its checks, and faults can occur anywhere in that circuit. The circuit-level distance is the fewest faults, for a given circuit and fault model, that cause a logical error without setting off any detector. For the [[144,12,12]] gross code and their syndrome circuit, Bravyi et al. at IBM (Nature 2024) proved this distance is at most 10 and conjectured that it equals 10; Cross, He, Rall and Yoder (2024) reported 10 from numerical evidence. An exhaustive search, run by two separately written programs, found no such set of 9 or fewer faults, for their circuit and fault model with 12 noisy rounds followed by 2 noiseless ones, in both memory bases; explicit sets of 10 faults do cause one. The two programs share the same mathematics and inputs, so they check the implementation, not the method. The same value 10 holds for the CZ-compiled version of the circuit in the Tesseract decoder's test files, and the distance is 8 for both versions of the [[108,8,10]] circuit. The Tesseract files are from Aghababaie Beni, Higgott and Shutty and the tesseract-decoder project, whose earlier MaxSAT values 6 and 8 for two smaller circuits were reproduced as controls. Webster, Jacob and Higgott earlier filtered the detector error model by detector type, the idea behind one of the search's reductions, and benchmarked distance solvers on these circuits; the solvers that certify the minimum completed only on the [[72,12,6]] circuit. We found no earlier exhaustive proof that 10 is the minimum. It is a search result, not an independently checkable certificate, and not yet reviewed by an expert.

Measurable chromatic numbers above Davies's Table 1

published 18 Oct 20267

Color d-dimensional space so that no two points exactly 1 apart share a color, where each color class must be a measurable set. If every measurable set with no two points 1 apart has density at most B, at least 1/B colors are needed. James Davies's preprint (arXiv:2610.12301, Table 1) gave such bounds in each dimension. Adding a few inequalities, mostly the triangle inequality, of the kind DeCorte, de Oliveira Filho and Vallentin used earlier (Math. Program. 2022) to Davies's linear program raises his lower bounds in seven dimensions. Each new bound comes with an exact certificate accepted by three checking programs in rigorous interval arithmetic, one of them written blind by a separate AI agent; the bounds also rest on a lemma proved in the accompanying note. Certificates for d = 15, 16, 18, 20, 22 and 23 are also in the package; two of the three checkers have accepted them. The same numbers bound the ordinary chromatic number only if Davies's Theorem 1.1 holds (preprint, not yet refereed). Not yet reviewed by a mathematician.

Exact distances of five quantum codes (two posted earlier by DistQLDPC)

published 14 Oct 20264

A quantum code's distance is the fewest qubit errors that can corrupt the stored data without being detected. Cain et al. (2026) printed upper bounds of 16 and 20 for two codes, and Xu et al. (2024) printed 16 and 20 for two more; for the second of those, Webster et al. later found a logical of weight 16. For Panteleev and Kalachev's Example 4, decoder searches found nothing below 20, but no bound was proved. An exhaustive computer search gives the exact values. For the two Xu et al. codes, Yu-Fang Chen's public DistQLDPC repository had already posted distance 16 on 11 Oct 2026, three days before our record, which wrongly called all five values new (correction). Our values for those two agree with theirs and are an independent recomputation. It is a search result, not an independently checkable certificate, and not yet reviewed by an expert.

Chromatic number of 3D space (conditional)

published 10 Oct 20263

Color all of space so that no two points exactly 1 apart share a color. How many colors are needed? Assuming one theorem from OpenAI's proof that the plane needs at least 6, at least 7. A written proof, not a computer certificate, and not yet reviewed by a mathematician. For 4D and up, James Davies proved stronger results unconditionally two days earlier. On 11 October 2026, Koshelev, Kupavskii and Voronov submitted “Ordinary and measurable colorings of Euclidean three-space” (arXiv:2610.18907). Assuming OpenAI's Theorem 2.3, they prove the stronger statement that the ordinary and measurable chromatic numbers of three-dimensional space are equal, with both between 7 and 15.

In progress

The stages below describe the intended publication process. The packages linked above are public with DOIs; their checking methods and limitations are described individually.

1. FoundThe system found a candidate and it passed a first rigorous computer check.
2. VerifiedIndependent checkers, a formally verified checker or Lean proof where possible, hostile AI review, and a literature check repeated just before release.
3. PublicThe note and any accompanying code are public with a DOI. Corrections are recorded and dated.#188, #189, 31, 228, 276
4. arXivA written paper on arXiv, ideally with a professional mathematician as co-author.
5. Peer-reviewedAccepted by a journal after independent review.

More results are in preparation. Stage 3 means the package is public with a DOI. A result is marked expert-checked when an independent mathematician has checked it; none has that mark yet.

Caught before publishing

Things that looked new or true at first, until the checks said otherwise. Each claim listed here was caught before release; in one case the underlying computation was later published as a reproduction of the known result. They are listed because catching them is the point. Three earlier postings we missed were found only after publishing; they are on the corrections page. Items about unpublished work will be added when that work is published.

  • A quantum code distance already found by Liang et al. (14 Oct 2026). The [[248,10]] code from the same 10,000-atom paper had been solved exactly in 2025 (arXiv:2503.03827). The novelty claim was dropped before release; the computation stays in the package as a reproduction. Evidence
  • A claimed 63 for #188 (2 Oct 2026). The search had graded itself on a sample of lines; the true figure was at least 68. Never announced. Evidence
  • A 5-chromatic unit-distance graph in a small disk (20 Sep 2026) was already beaten in the Polymath16 project in 2021. Evidence
  • A theorem overtaken by Tomon's paper (arXiv:2610.03517, 2 Oct 2026), found before release. Dropped. Evidence
  • Higher-dimension bounds overtaken by Davies's paper (arXiv:2610.12301, 8 Oct 2026), found by a literature check just before release. The 3D note was cut to dimension 3. Evidence
  • An exact value already proved by Cabello in 2012 (arXiv:1112.5149). Dropped. Evidence
  • Table entries first counted as new that followed from graphs Cherkashin, Kulikov and Raigorodskii had already published. Evidence
