A triangle-free 5-chromatic graph in 3D with 31 points

Pick some points in ordinary three-dimensional space and draw a line between every two points that are exactly 1 apart. Now color the points so that no line joins two points of the same color. How many colors do you need? This page is about a set of 31 points that needs 5 colors, even though no three of the points are all exactly 1 apart from each other.2

61 points→31 points

Stage 3, published 8 Oct 2026: doi:10.5281/zenodo.23241767.10 The files below also let you check it. Not yet reviewed by a professional mathematician.

The problem in plain words

The points and the lines between them form a unit-distance graph. Its chromatic number is the fewest colors that keep every line's two ends different. Here the graph is faithful: two points are joined exactly when they are 1 apart, with no exceptions.2

Three points all 1 apart form a triangle, and a triangle already needs 3 colors. A graph with no triangles has to force its colors in a less direct way, so triangle-free graphs that need many colors are harder to find. Small examples matter because they are easier to check, to understand and to build on.

In 2026 Aubrey de Grey gave a triangle-free unit-distance graph in three dimensions that needs 5 colors, with 61 points.1

What is new

A faithful, triangle-free unit-distance graph in three dimensions with 31 points and 96 lines that needs exactly 5 colors. It is also 5-critical: remove any point or any line and 4 colors are enough.24 Variants with 33 and 35 points also exist.4

István Tomon has since shown that such graphs exist even in the plane, with no short cycles at all.9 His construction is probabilistic and gives no explicit graph or point count. What this page adds is an explicit, small example in three dimensions.

I do not claim that 31 is the smallest possible. Every triangle-free graph that needs 5 colors has at least 22 points, so the smallest such set in space has between 22 and 31 points.74

How it was checked

  • Certificate: an interval-arithmetic test (the Krawczyk test) proves that exact points exist within 10−40 of the listed coordinates, and SAT solvers show that 4 colors are not enough. Seventeen deliberately corrupted inputs were all rejected.4
  • Blind verifier: a second program, written from a short description of the claim without seeing the certifier's code, reached the same result with different arithmetic.4
  • Hostile review: a separate review repeated the interval test with a different setup and checked the coloring claims with its own search. It found no error.4
  • Re-check: on 7 October 2026 all the verifiers were run again and passed, and the literature was checked again.5
  • The checker on this page repeats the essential checks in about 25 seconds.2

All the verifiers were written with the same AI tool (Claude Code), in separate sessions. That is not independence in the sense of different authors, which is why checks by others are very welcome.8 To my knowledge, no mathematician has checked the result yet.

Check it yourself

Download the files below into one folder and run:2

pip install mpmath
python check_31.py        # about 25 seconds

The last line says PASS or FAIL. The checker uses only Python's standard library and mpmath. It checks every line's length exactly or with rigorous interval arithmetic, checks that there are no triangles, and searches every possible 4-coloring without a SAT solver. It passes from a clean copy in under a minute on an ordinary PC. (evidence)

Note: how the construction works

Mycielski's construction turns a triangle-free graph that needs 4 colors into a triangle-free graph that needs 5. For every point v, add a "shadow" point joined to all of v's neighbors. Then add one more point, the apex, joined to all the shadows.3 The hard part in geometry is placing the shadows and the apex so that every new line has length exactly 1.

De Grey's idea is to place each shadow at the mirror image of its point in the plane through that point's neighbors. The neighbors are the same distance from a point and from its mirror image, so the shadow's lines have length 1 automatically.1 What remains is the apex, which must be exactly 1 from every shadow.

Here is the observation that makes the apex easy. Put all the starting points on a sphere of radius R. Then each shadow lands on the line from the center through its point, at distance |R − 1/R| from the center. That distance is exactly 1 when R is the golden ratio φ ≈ 1.618 or 1/φ ≈ 0.618. On such a sphere every shadow is 1 from the center, so the center itself can be the apex.2

That leaves one task: find a triangle-free graph that needs 4 colors and can be drawn on the sphere of radius 1/φ with all its lines of length exactly 1. A 15-point graph from Goedgebeur's lists works.64 It cannot be drawn this way in a flat plane, because its four-sided loops would have to be flat rhombuses, and together they force two joined points to coincide. On a sphere those loops do not have to close up flat, and that is what leaves room for the graph.4

The positions were found numerically and then proved by the interval test. The result has 15 + 15 + 1 = 31 points: the base graph, its shadows, and the apex at the center.4
