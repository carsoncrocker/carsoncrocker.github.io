A 5-chromatic graph on a sphere with 228 points

Take a sphere of radius about 0.951 and pick some points on it. Draw a line between every two points that are exactly 1 apart, measured straight through space. Color the points so that no line joins two points of the same color. This page is about 228 such points that need 5 colors.2

372 points→228 points

Stage 3, published 8 Oct 2026: doi:10.5281/zenodo.23241819.7 The files below also let you check it. Not yet reviewed by a professional mathematician.

The problem in plain words

The points and the lines between them form a unit-distance graph, and its chromatic number is the fewest colors that keep the two ends of every line different. The graph is faithful: two points are joined exactly when they are 1 apart, with no exceptions.2

The radius is cos(π/10) ≈ 0.95106. This is the sphere through the 12 corners of an icosahedron whose edges have length 1, so the icosahedron is a natural place to start.2 On a sphere, as in the plane, one asks how many colors are needed, and a small graph that needs 5 colors is the simplest kind of proof that 4 are not enough. Small examples are easier to check and to understand.

Voronov, Neopryatnaya and Dergachev built a 5-chromatic unit-distance graph on this sphere with 372 points. They also remark that deleting any one point keeps 5 colors necessary, which already gives 371.13

What is new

A faithful 5-chromatic unit-distance graph on this sphere with 228 points and 910 lines, with exact coordinates. It is vertex-critical: remove any one point and 4 colors are enough. A second example with 231 points also exists.23

I do not claim that 228 is the smallest possible; the smallest size is not known.

How it was checked

  • Certificate: every point and every distance is computed exactly, in a number field of degree 8. The pairs exactly 1 apart are exactly the 910 listed lines. A SAT solver's proof that 4 colors are not enough (a DRAT certificate) was checked with drat-trim, and a 4-coloring is stored for the graph with each point removed.3
  • Blind verifier: a separate program, written from the data without reading any project code, recomputed the geometry and the lines exactly. Three SAT solvers found no 4-coloring, and its own proof passed drat-trim.4
  • Hostile review: found no mathematical error, and confirmed that the graph is not a piece of the 372-point construction. The two have at most the 12 icosahedron points in common.4
  • Re-check: on 7 October 2026 the verifiers were run again and passed, and the literature was checked again.5
  • The checker on this page repeats the exact checks and the proof check in about a minute.2

All the verifiers were written with the same AI tool (Claude Code), in separate sessions. That is not independence in the sense of different authors, which is why checks by others are very welcome.6 To my knowledge, no mathematician has checked the result yet.

Check it yourself

Download the files below into one folder and run:2

pip install python-flint python-sat    # python-sat is optional
python verify_standalone.py           # about 1 minute

The last line says ALL CHECKS PASS or lists what failed. The checker computes exactly, in the number field, that every point is on the sphere and that the pairs at distance exactly 1 are exactly the listed lines. It then rebuilds the coloring question from the line list and checks the bundled proof that it has no 4-coloring. It uses drat-trim if that is installed, and otherwise a built-in checker written in plain Python. It passes from a clean copy in about a minute and a half on an ordinary PC. (evidence)

Note: how the construction works

Start with the 12 corners of the icosahedron with edges of length 1. They lie on this sphere, and its 30 edges are lines of the graph. Add a second copy, turned about the axis through the center of one face by an angle θ with cos θ = (3√5 − 5)/4, about 64.72°. This angle is chosen so that a corner and its turned copy are exactly 1 apart.23

Then grow the set. Around any point, the points of the sphere at distance 1 form a circle. Two such circles usually meet in two points, and each of those is exactly 1 from both starting points. Adding these meeting points again and again grew the set to 1,008 points.23

A large set like that needs 5 colors, but most of its points are not needed for that. Computer searches removed points while keeping 4 colors insufficient: 1,008 to 407, then 292, then 231 and 228.3

Every step is an exact construction, so all coordinates are exact numbers in one field of degree 8. That is what lets the checker decide "exactly 1 apart" without rounding.23
